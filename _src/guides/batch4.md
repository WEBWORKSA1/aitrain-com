=== write-an-ai-use-policy | How to write a one-page AI use policy (with template) | AI for Leaders | 8 | A practical, plain-language AI acceptable-use policy template covering approved tools, data rules, human review, disclosure and reporting.
Staff are already using AI, with or without permission. A short, clear policy protects your organisation while letting people get the benefit. Long policies go unread; one page is enough to start.

## Key takeaways

- Cover five things: approved tools, data rules, human review, disclosure, and who to contact.
- Write in plain language with examples.
- Pair the policy with an approved tool and short training.
- Review it every quarter while the technology moves quickly.

## The template

Copy and adapt. Replace bracketed text.

---

**[Organisation] — AI Use Policy (version [x], [month year])**

**Purpose.** We encourage staff to use AI tools to work more effectively, within the rules below.

**1. Approved tools.** You may use: [list approved tools and plans]. To request a new tool, contact [role/email alias]. Do not use personal AI accounts for work involving internal information.

**2. Data rules.**
- *Allowed in approved tools:* internal documents and non-public business information needed for the task.
- *Never enter in any AI tool (unless a specific approved process exists):* [personal data about customers, patients, students or staff; passwords and credentials; payment details; confidential client information covered by contract; legally privileged material].
- When possible, remove names and identifying details.

**3. Human review.** You are responsible for anything you produce with AI. Check facts, figures, citations and tone before sharing. AI must not make final decisions about people (hiring, performance, eligibility) without human review.

**4. Disclosure.** Tell [customers/readers/clients] when content is substantially generated by AI where [our standards/regulations] require it, and always when asked.

**5. Intellectual property.** Do not ask AI to reproduce copyrighted material. Check AI-generated content for similarity to existing work before publishing externally.

**6. Security.** Be cautious with AI tools connected to email, files or systems. Review any action before it is taken. Report suspicious behaviour to [security contact].

**7. Questions and incidents.** Contact [role]. Report mistakes quickly — we treat honest reports as learning, not blame.

---

## Making it stick

- **Launch with training.** Walk through the policy with real examples in under an hour.
- **Give an approved option on day one.** Policies without a sanctioned tool push usage underground.
- **Add a FAQ.** Collect real questions in the first month and answer them in writing.
- **Name an owner.** Someone must review it quarterly.

## Regulatory context

Some jurisdictions have specific AI rules. For example, the EU AI Act includes an AI-literacy obligation for providers and deployers of AI systems; see our [AI literacy checklist](eu-ai-act-ai-literacy-checklist.html). Sector rules — healthcare, finance, education, public sector — may add requirements.

*This template is general information and not legal advice. Have it reviewed by someone qualified for your jurisdiction and sector.*

=== eu-ai-act-ai-literacy-checklist | AI literacy under the EU AI Act: a practical checklist for organisations | Responsible AI | 8 | What the EU AI Act's AI-literacy provision asks of organisations that use AI, and a practical checklist for building and documenting staff AI literacy.
The EU Artificial Intelligence Act includes a short but far-reaching provision on AI literacy. Article 4 asks providers and deployers of AI systems to take measures to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy among staff and others dealing with AI systems on their behalf. The obligation has applied since 2 February 2025.

This guide translates that into practical steps. It is general information, not legal advice.

## Key takeaways

- It applies broadly — not only to developers of high-risk systems, but to organisations that deploy AI tools in their work.
- "Sufficient" depends on people's roles, their technical background and the context in which AI is used.
- There is no single mandated course or certificate; what matters is proportionate, documented measures.
- Good AI-literacy training also improves productivity — compliance and capability point the same way.

## Who is in scope?

Organisations that provide AI systems, and organisations that deploy them under their authority, where those systems are placed on the market or used in the EU. In practice, many businesses outside the EU that serve EU customers or have EU operations also pay attention to it.

## What "AI literacy" means in practice

The Act describes AI literacy as the skills, knowledge and understanding that allow people to make informed use of AI systems and be aware of their opportunities, risks and possible harms. A useful breakdown for training:

1. **Concepts:** what AI and generative AI are, in plain terms.
2. **Capabilities and limits:** strengths, hallucinations, bias, outdated knowledge.
3. **Your tools:** the specific systems your organisation uses and their intended purpose.
4. **Data and privacy:** what can and cannot be entered.
5. **Human oversight:** when and how to review, override or escalate.
6. **Role-specific risks:** e.g. HR decisions, customer communications, health or financial contexts.

## The checklist

- [ ] Inventory AI systems in use, including AI features inside existing software.
- [ ] Map which roles use which systems, and for what.
- [ ] Define proportionate literacy levels per role (e.g. all staff / regular users / high-impact users / technical owners).
- [ ] Deliver foundation training for all staff who use AI.
- [ ] Deliver role-specific training for higher-impact uses.
- [ ] Publish an [AI use policy](write-an-ai-use-policy.html) and make it easy to find.
- [ ] Record who completed which training and when.
- [ ] Provide a channel for questions and incident reports.
- [ ] Refresh training when tools, uses or rules change, and at least annually.
- [ ] Review the programme with legal or compliance advisers.

## Documentation tips

Keep a simple register: system, owner, user roles, training provided, completion dates, and review date. If asked how your organisation meets its AI-literacy duty, that register — plus training materials and the policy — is your evidence.

## Proportionality

A ten-person marketing agency using a general-purpose assistant does not need the same programme as a hospital deploying AI in triage. Scale effort to risk and role.

=== ai-for-nurses-and-healthcare-teams | AI for nurses and healthcare teams: safe, practical uses | Role Guides | 9 | Practical, low-risk ways nurses and healthcare teams can use AI for education, communication and admin — and the patient-safety and privacy lines not to cross.
Healthcare workers are among the busiest professionals anywhere, and much of their time goes to documentation, education and communication. AI can help with parts of that — but the stakes are higher than in most jobs, so the boundaries need to be clear.

## Key takeaways

- Never enter identifiable patient information into an AI tool your organisation hasn't approved for that purpose.
- AI does not replace clinical judgement, policies, or professional standards from your regulator.
- The safest high-value uses are education, plain-language communication drafts and non-clinical admin.
- Verify any clinical fact against approved sources before acting on it.

## Lower-risk, high-value uses

### 1. Learning and revision
- "Explain the difference between [concept A] and [concept B] at the level of a new graduate nurse, then give me five quiz questions."
- Summarise a published guideline you've pasted in (not from memory) into a one-page study sheet.
- Create flashcards for medication classes — then check every card against your drug reference.

### 2. Patient-education drafts (de-identified)
- "Rewrite this discharge instruction at a grade 6 reading level, keeping every instruction and warning." Then compare line by line against the original; nothing clinical should be added or dropped.
- Draft explanations in plain language for common procedures, to be reviewed against approved materials.

### 3. Non-clinical admin
- Drafting staff memos, rota announcements, meeting agendas and education-session plans.
- Turning in-service notes into a handout.
- Writing emails to vendors or other departments.

### 4. Quality-improvement support
- Structuring a QI project plan, aims and measures.
- Drafting survey questions for staff feedback.

## Higher-risk uses — only with organisational approval

- Any tool that processes patient records, including ambient documentation or note-generation tools.
- Clinical decision support.
- Anything that summarises a specific patient's history.

These can be valuable when deployed by your organisation with appropriate privacy, security, validation and policy controls. They are not something to improvise with a personal account.

## Lines not to cross

- No names, dates of birth, record numbers, room numbers, or rare-condition details that could identify a person.
- No relying on AI for dosages, drug interactions, or clinical decisions.
- No AI-generated content in the health record unless your organisation has an approved process.
- Follow your regulator's standards on documentation, confidentiality and professional accountability; you remain accountable for what you document and do.

## Prompts to start with

```
You are a clinical nurse educator. Create a 20-minute in-service plan on [topic] for long-term-care staff with mixed experience. Include learning objectives, a short case scenario (fictional, no real patient details), three discussion questions and a five-question quiz with answers. Flag anything that should be checked against our facility policy.
```

```
Rewrite the following education leaflet at a grade 6 reading level. Keep every instruction, warning and number unchanged. Return the rewrite and then a list of any terms you simplified.
[paste approved leaflet text]
```

## For managers and educators

Healthcare teams benefit from training that starts with privacy and verification habits and then moves to specific, approved use cases. Our [team training](../teams.html) can be tailored to clinical education and administrative teams.

=== ai-for-teachers | AI for teachers: lesson planning, differentiation and feedback | Role Guides | 8 | How teachers can use AI to plan lessons, differentiate materials, write rubrics and give feedback faster — plus guidance on student privacy and academic integrity.
Teachers spend evenings and weekends on planning, adapting materials and marking. AI can return some of that time, as long as student privacy and professional judgement stay at the centre.

## Key takeaways

- Use AI for preparation: plans, variations, examples, rubrics and question banks.
- Keep student names and personal data out of tools your school hasn't approved.
- Check every fact and example; AI-generated content can contain errors.
- Talk openly with students about how AI may and may not be used in your class.

## Lesson planning

```
You are an experienced [grade/subject] teacher. Plan a [length] lesson on [topic] aligned to this learning goal: [goal or curriculum expectation].
Include: a hook, direct instruction (key points only), a pair activity, a check for understanding, and an exit ticket. Note likely misconceptions.
```

Adapt to your class — you know your students; the AI doesn't.

## Differentiation

- "Rewrite this reading passage at three levels: below grade, at grade, and enrichment. Keep the key facts identical."
- "Create a scaffolded version of this worksheet with sentence starters and a worked example."
- "Suggest two extension tasks for students who finish early."

## Question banks and practice

- Generate varied practice questions with answers — then solve a few yourself to check accuracy, especially in maths and science.
- Ask for common wrong answers and why students choose them; useful for multiple-choice distractors.

## Rubrics and feedback

```
Create a four-level rubric for [assignment] with criteria: [list]. Use student-friendly language. Describe what each level looks like concretely.
```

For feedback, paste anonymised work (no names) and ask for two strengths and one next step tied to your rubric. Edit before sharing — your voice and knowledge of the student matter.

## Academic integrity

- State your class policy clearly: where AI is allowed (brainstorming? feedback on drafts?) and where it isn't.
- Design some tasks that reward process: drafts, reflections, in-class work, oral explanations.
- Be careful with AI-detection tools; they can produce false accusations. Use conversations and process evidence instead.

## Privacy

Follow your school or district policy. Avoid entering student names, grades, IEP details or behaviour records into unapproved tools.

=== ai-for-small-business-owners | AI for small business owners: 12 practical uses that save hours | Role Guides | 9 | Twelve concrete ways small business owners can use AI for marketing, customer service, admin and planning — with prompts and the mistakes to avoid.
Small businesses gain more from AI than almost anyone, because the owner usually does every job. The goal isn't to adopt everything; it's to find the three or four uses that give you back several hours a week.

## Key takeaways

- Start with writing-heavy tasks you already do every week.
- Use built-in AI features in tools you already pay for before buying new ones.
- Keep customer personal data out of tools you haven't vetted.
- Always review anything customers will see.

## Marketing

1. **Social posts from one idea.** "Turn this update into an Instagram caption, a Facebook post and a short email paragraph. Friendly, local tone."
2. **Product or service descriptions.** Use [few-shot examples](few-shot-prompting-examples.html) from your best existing descriptions to keep your voice.
3. **Review replies.** "Draft a reply to this 3-star review that thanks them, addresses the slow-service point without excuses, and invites them back."
4. **Simple content calendar.** "Plan 8 weeks of posts for a [type of business] in [season], mixing tips, behind-the-scenes and offers."

## Customer service

5. **FAQ page.** Paste common customer emails and ask for a clear FAQ.
6. **Reply templates.** Build a small library for common questions and edit each time.
7. **Translate.** Draft replies in a customer's language, and have them checked if it matters.

## Admin and operations

8. **Job ads.** Write inclusive job postings and interview questions.
9. **Policies and procedures.** Turn your notes into a clear opening/closing checklist or returns policy.
10. **Supplier emails.** Polite chasers, quote requests and negotiations.

## Planning and money

11. **Pricing thinking.** "Here are my costs and competitor prices [your numbers]. What pricing options should I consider and what are the trade-offs?" Do the arithmetic in a spreadsheet.
12. **Business plan sections.** Use [prompt chaining](prompt-chaining-for-long-tasks.html) to draft a plan section by section.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Publishing AI text without reading it — it can invent offers, prices or policies.
- Using AI-generated "statistics" in marketing without checking them.
- Letting every post sound generic — add a real detail from your business each time.
- Putting customer lists or payment details into unvetted tools.

| Tue | Build five customer-reply templates |
| Wed | Turn your notes into an FAQ page |
| Thu | Rewrite your two weakest product descriptions |

AI for teachers: lesson planning, differentiation and feedback

How teachers can use AI to plan lessons, differentiate materials, write rubrics and give feedback faster — plus guidance on student privacy and academic integrity.

By the AiTrain editorial team · Reviewed under our editorial standards

Teachers spend evenings and weekends on planning, adapting materials and marking. AI can return some of that time, as long as student privacy and professional judgement stay at the centre.

Key takeaways

  • Use AI for preparation: plans, variations, examples, rubrics and question banks.
  • Keep student names and personal data out of tools your school hasn't approved.
  • Check every fact and example; AI-generated content can contain errors.
  • Talk openly with students about how AI may and may not be used in your class.

Lesson planning

You are an experienced [grade/subject] teacher. Plan a [length] lesson on [topic] aligned to this learning goal: [goal or curriculum expectation].
Include: a hook, direct instruction (key points only), a pair activity, a check for understanding, and an exit ticket. Note likely misconceptions.

Adapt to your class — you know your students; the AI doesn't.

Differentiation

  • "Rewrite this reading passage at three levels: below grade, at grade, and enrichment. Keep the key facts identical."
  • "Create a scaffolded version of this worksheet with sentence starters and a worked example."
  • "Suggest two extension tasks for students who finish early."

Question banks and practice

  • Generate varied practice questions with answers — then solve a few yourself to check accuracy, especially in maths and science.
  • Ask for common wrong answers and why students choose them; useful for multiple-choice distractors.

Rubrics and feedback

Create a four-level rubric for [assignment] with criteria: [list]. Use student-friendly language. Describe what each level looks like concretely.

For feedback, paste anonymised work (no names) and ask for two strengths and one next step tied to your rubric. Edit before sharing — your voice and knowledge of the student matter.

Academic integrity

  • State your class policy clearly: where AI is allowed (brainstorming? feedback on drafts?) and where it isn't.
  • Design some tasks that reward process: drafts, reflections, in-class work, oral explanations.
  • Be careful with AI-detection tools; they can produce false accusations. Use conversations and process evidence instead.

Privacy

Follow your school or district policy. Avoid entering student names, grades, IEP details or behaviour records into unapproved tools.
